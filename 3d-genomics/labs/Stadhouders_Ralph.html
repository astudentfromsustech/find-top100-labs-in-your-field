Temporal multi-omics analysis reveals an unappreciated key role for common γ-chain cytokines in early ILC2 activationlast author

2026-03-19 · preprint / no venue on record · 0 citations · doi:10.1183/23120541.lsc-2026.yi504

Group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2) may drive the pathophysiology of asthma. Classically, ILC2s are thought to require epithelial alarmins (IL-33/TSLP) for activation and induction of a potent effector cell program. In addition, common γ-chain cytokines (IL-2/IL-7) promote survival and further augment ILC2 activity. To identify early molecular circuits driving ILC2 activation, we performed multi-omics analysis (i.e. RNA-seq, ATAC-seq and Hi-C) of in vitro-activated primary human blood ILC2s to analyze transcriptional and epigenetic dynamics. RNA-Seq and ATAC-Seq revealed extensive epigenomic reprogramming of resting ILC2s within the first 24h after cytokine stimulation, involving key metabolic and inflammatory genes. Strikingly, these dynamics were primarily driven by common γ-chain cytokines – including the immediate induction of a transient early wave (at 1h) of AP-1 and EGR transcription factor expression that precedes alarmin receptor induction (at 4h). Subsequently, alarmin stimulation activated surprisingly few additional genes, instead primarily increasing the expression of inflammatory genes already activated by IL-2/IL-7. Our findings suggest a key role for common γ-chain cytokines in the induction of early ILC2 activation, acting as a primary signal to establish an epigenomic landscape that sensitizes cells to alarmins. In contrast to popular belief, alarmins did not appear to act as drivers but instead as amplifiers of inflammatory gene transcription during human ILC2 activation.

Decoding the genetic predisposition to asthma using epigenomicslast author

2024-03-15 · preprint / no venue on record · 0 citations · doi:10.1183/23120541.lsc-2024.75

Introduction: Asthma, an inflammatory airway disease, results from intricate interactions between genetics and environmental factors. High medical costs are mostly incurred by the 5-10% of patients requiring intensive treatment due to poor symptom control. Aims and objectives: We aim to highlight targets for phenotype-specific and personalized treatment of (severe) asthma by studying the genetic variants underlying asthma predisposition. Methods: We use the results of genome-wide association studies that analyze genotypes of large cohorts of asthma patients. These studies identify up to 179 asthma-associated loci in which ~99% of genetic variants reside in non-coding genomic regions—thereby largely obscuring direct links to susceptibility genes. To find cell-type specific links between genetic variants and genes, we use an integrated epigenomics approach based on active chromatin signatures (ATAC-seq/ChIP-seq data), 3D chromatin folding (Hi-C data) and gene expression patterns (RNA-seq data). Results: Systematically intersecting asthma-associated genetic variants with features of gene regulatory activity of 47 cell types revealed clusters of genetic loci linked to disease-relevant cell types. These included well-known gene-cell type pairs, e.g. the interleukin 33 locus and epithelial cells, and new associations involving mast cells, B or T cells, and even early blood cell precursors. We could also link genetic variants in large gene deserts to GATA3, which encodes a transcription factor driving inflammatory cytokine production in T helper 2 cells. Conclusions: We present a systematic way to translate genetic associations into tractable hypotheses, uncovering potentially new biological mechanisms relevant for asthma pathogenesis.
